Building a drinks menu for a restaurant or cafe

Notes from behind the bar on writing a drinks list that fits the food and holds up on a busy night.

5 min read · Notes from Dissolved Solids

A restaurant's drinks list is often the last thing written, after the food is settled and the opening date is close. We think that order is right. The drinks should answer the food, and it's hard to answer something that isn't written yet. The trouble is the time left over. When it runs short, the list gets filled from a supplier's catalogue and ends up longer than anyone on the floor can make well.

These notes come from writing the list at Dissolved Solids and from running Soluble Solids with no list at all. Most of it carries over to a restaurant or cafe.

Start from the food

Read the food menu before you think about a single drink. Look at where the heat sits, and at how much of the menu is fried or cooked in coconut milk. A menu heavy on sambal wants different drinks from one built around steamed fish.

Our rough rules for Malaysian food are in the pairing guide, and the one we come back to most is that rich food wants acid. A kitchen cooking rendang and nasi lemak can lean on sours and spritzes, since sharpness and bubbles both cut through coconut and fat. A cafe selling cakes and kaya toast has more in common with a coffee bar, and coffee drinks or something bitter will sit better next to the sugar.

Keep the list short

We keep the list at Dissolved Solids short on purpose, and the reasons apply to a restaurant even more. The person making your drinks may also be running food and taking orders. Every drink on the list needs its own bottles on the shelf and a place in someone's memory. One that gets ordered twice a week will taste a bit different each time, because nobody's hands are used to it.

We'd start with a handful of drinks the team can make well on a full night, and let the list grow only when there's a reason to.

Write it for whoever is on shift

The list has to work on the night your best bartender is off. That changes what goes on it. A drink with a long build or a fiddly garnish is fine in a bar with someone dedicated to it. In a busy dining room it slows the floor, and it comes out differently depending on who made it.

The way around this is to move the hard part of each drink off the shift. Anything that doesn't change in the bottle (spirits, syrups, liqueurs, bitters) can be mixed ahead. Citrus and soda go in when the drink is made, since both fade once they're mixed. It's the same split we describe in batching cocktails for a party, and it holds at a restaurant's scale. Someone careful does the mixing once, earlier in the day, and the person on shift only has to pour and finish the drink.

That made-ahead part is the premix. It keeps a drink the same no matter who is working, and it makes service quick enough for a dining room.

Give the alcohol-free side real thought

In Malaysia a good share of any dining room won't be drinking alcohol. In a halal kitchen nobody will, and the alcohol-free list carries the whole drinks side of the meal by itself.

We treat these drinks the way we treat cocktails. The ones that disappoint tend to be only sweet. The ones that work have acid, and often a little bitterness or salt, so they taste finished next to food. There's more on this in our piece on the savoury mocktail. Serve them in the same glassware as the cocktails, so the table looks like one table.

Use what the kitchen already has

Most kitchens here already stock things a bar pays extra for: pandan, lemongrass, calamansi, ginger, gula melaka, kopi. Drinks made from them taste like they belong with the food, because they share ingredients with it, and there's less to buy only for the bar.

The Kopi Sour at Dissolved Solids is a small example. Apart from the whisky, it's kopi-O, gula melaka and lemon, which any Malaysian kitchen already has.

It's also worth asking the chef what gets thrown away each week. Citrus peels and herb stems can go into syrups instead of the bin.

Price it with the food

In our experience, a drink that costs more than the main course makes people hesitate, whatever the bottle cost you. We'd look at the food menu's prices first and fit the drinks around them. If a drink can't be made at a price that sits with the food, it's usually the wrong drink for that restaurant, and a simpler one will do the job better.

A premix helps here too. A drink that's mixed ahead costs the same every time it's poured, which makes it far easier to price.

Test it with the people who will make it

Before a drink goes on the list, hand it to whoever will be making it on a busy night. Let them make it in the time they'll actually have, and taste it next to the dish it's meant for. If it only works when the owner makes it slowly, it isn't ready.

Then watch the first few weeks. Drinks nobody orders can come off. The ones the floor team keeps recommending usually point to what the next drink should be.

Frequently asked questions

Where should a restaurant start when building a drinks menu?

With the food menu. Look at where the heat and the richness sit, then pick drinks that answer them. Rich, coconut-heavy dishes usually want something with acid or bubbles. Our pairing notes for Malaysian food go dish by dish.

How many drinks should a restaurant drinks menu have?

Fewer than most people expect. We'd start with a handful the team can make well on a full night, and add one only when there's a clear reason, like guests asking for something that isn't there. A short list means each drink gets made often enough to stay consistent.

What is a cocktail premix?

The part of a drink that can be made ahead: the spirits, syrups, liqueurs and bitters, mixed in a larger batch. At service the staff pour it and add the fresh part, such as citrus or soda. It keeps each drink the same whoever is on shift.

Can a halal restaurant have a proper drinks menu?

Yes. An alcohol-free list can get the same care as a cocktail list. Acid and a little bitterness or salt make the drinks taste finished, and kitchen ingredients like pandan, calamansi, ginger and kopi tie them to the food.
